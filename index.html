How I Apply SPC on the Molding Floor

I don't look at Statistical Process Control (SPC) as a bunch of old math or historical charts to show auditors after the run is finished. I use SPC live, shot-by-shot, to track the machine's true "vital signs." I do not just measure the final molded component; I track the critical, interconnected parameters that govern its volumetric shrink rate and physical size.

Cushion Size Monitoring the exact point where the screw stops every single cycle to ensure uniform melt packing density.
Peak Injection Pressure Tracking material resistance to capture viscosity drops or shifts caused by contaminated or unverified raw resin lots.
Fill & Recovery Time Measuring precise multi-second windows to guarantee polymer chains grow the identical shot size cycle-to-cycle.

Processing the Atmosphere, Not Just the Plastic

When I set up a validated Class A process, I aim to have SPC data running flat right dead-center of the control limits. If it drifts, it means something has changed. I don't just troubleshoot the press itself; I monitor the entire living thermodynamic system because everything on a manufacturing floor is interconnected:

  • The Ambient Environment: If an operator brings a fan over to the machine or a bay door gets opened, the cool air and/or humidity drafts over the molding machine area, sweat waterlines or the feed throat. This changes the steel temperature, altering how the plastic solidifies, and blows out gap-and-flush tolerances.
  • Hydraulic & Chiller Subsystems: If cooling water drops in pressure and allows hydraulic oil to thin out or thicken, your valves lose their capability to react with repeatable speeds. This immediately throws off fill times and shot consistency.
  • Servo & Motor Kinematics: On hybrid and electric presses, operating temperatures should be logged. When they heat up under high-volume runs, their internal mechanical resistance changes, causing subtle velocity lags.

My bottom line for any interviewer is simple: I don't just process the plastic; I process the entire atmosphere around the machine to guarantee as close to 100% repeatability from the first shot to the last as possible. If my target fill time or recovery time drifts past my hard boundaries, the machine stays down until the root cause is fixed.

Tooling Protection & The Twin-Brother & Sister Auxiliary Principle

Most plants make process sheets that do nothing but blindly imitate the screen settings on the machine. To me, that approach completely ignores the actual physics of molding. If you run a process without tracking your core and moving part pressures, your oil temps, cooling lines, as well as the ambient atmosphere, you are flying blind. When a plant forces a machine to run out-of-spec parameters to hit a short-sighted quota, they are doing something far worse than making bad parts—they are destroying the tooling incrementally.

Every cycle run with bad material viscosity or jacked-up pressures forces the mold halves to disinegrate harder than designed. Idealistically, these actions micro-crushes the parting lines, rounds off sharp steel corners, and eat away at core pins shot by shot. If a company pays me to work for them, maintaining near-zero tool destruction is the exact attitude that comes with the package.

The Thermolator Law: Twin Brother & Sister with Different Personalities

I am exceptionally strict about auxiliary equipment setup cell-to-cell. Once an auxiliary unit like a thermolator or chiller is used to establish a validated process, it must stay mapped to that press. Swapping equipment around like musical chairs kills process capability. If you want me to waste the company's money, this is how I advance solution-wise. If another machine needs a thermolator, buy one for that machine. Even if it is for a piece of auxiliary equipment, it should have its own thermolator.

Even if you pull an identical make and model from the maintenace department, tool room, or right out of a crated box, they are like **twin brothers & sisters—each has their own separate personality**. Internal scaling, pump pressure wear, and flow resistance vary unit-to-unit. Unless a unit physically breaks down or malfunctions on the floor, it stays locked to the press associated. This has to happen. If a swap needs to happen, it is treated as a major process shift that requires immediate verification against the master log.
